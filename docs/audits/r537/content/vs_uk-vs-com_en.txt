.uk vs .com
.uk vs .com: British Local Trust vs the Global Default
Which to pick
As with every ccTLD-versus-.com call, it comes down to where your users are. For a UK-focused business, .uk is near-mandatory: run by Nominet for over 25 years with registrations in the tens of millions, British consumers read .uk/.co.uk as the default signal of a local business — trust far beyond any new gTLD — and Google geo-associates .uk sites with the UK, a free local-SEO boost. Since 2014 you can register directly at the second level, and name.uk is shorter and more modern than name.co.uk; about $6 to register with the same renewal — honest ccTLD pricing. For a global audience, .com's recognition is irreplaceable — autocomplete reflex and the best long-term asset liquidity. Established UK brands with budget usually take both: .uk (plus .co.uk) as the local front door or defensively, .com as the international main site. Two .uk-specific cautions: Nominet requires a UK address for service, so overseas registrants must confirm their registrar proxies it; and if someone else holds the matching .co.uk, weigh the brand-confusion risk before committing. The test: users, logistics and compliance all in the UK → .uk delivers local trust .com can't; global market → .com remains the safety zone. The registries tell you how each behaves. .com is Verisign's zone, in the root since 1985 under an ICANN registry agreement most recently renewed in December 2024, with price terms fixed by amendment and no geographic meaning at all. .uk is the United Kingdom's country code, delegated in July 1985 — the same year — and managed by Nominet UK, an Oxford-based registry that runs it under UK policy rather than an ICANN gTLD contract; direct second-level .uk names sit alongside the long-established .co.uk and .org.uk third-level spaces, and the registry's rules, dispute service and pricing are set in Oxford, not California. For a business that matters in two ways. First, a .uk address is an unambiguous location signal to British customers and to search engines' country targeting, which a .com only achieves through explicit geotargeting settings. Second, because .uk is a ccTLD, changes in its policy or pricing follow Nominet's own processes and UK law, while .com's follow ICANN's — different kinds of predictability. Use-case split: a British retailer with UK-only delivery, a trades business serving one region, a UK charity, a professional practice regulated by a UK body, a brand whose customers will type .co.uk by reflex → .uk (or .co.uk); a company selling into the US or globally, a SaaS product with international pricing, a brand that will raise money from non-UK investors, an exporter → .com. Most UK companies of any size hold both and redirect one to the other.
